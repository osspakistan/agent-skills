# Pattern guide

Use this as a set of things to inspect, not a blacklist. A single occurrence rarely proves anything. Keep a pattern when it is precise, useful, quoted, required by the format, or genuinely sounds like the writer.

## Inflated claims and unsupported authority

- **Significance puffery:** "marks a pivotal moment," "stands as a testament," "plays a vital role," "underscores its importance," "sets the stage for," or claims that a small fact reflects a broad trend. State what happened and let the reader judge its importance.
- **Promotional gloss:** "nestled," "breathtaking," "vibrant," "stunning," "renowned," "groundbreaking," "must-visit," and similar praise without evidence. Replace it with concrete qualities, location, use, or outcomes.
- **Vague authority:** "experts say," "industry observers note," "studies show," or "widely regarded" without identifying a source. Name the source and what it found, or remove/qualify the claim. Never invent a citation.
- **Speculative gap filling:** guesses about someone's biography, motives, or circumstances, often wrapped in "details are scarce" or "maintains a low profile." State what is known, say what remains unknown when relevant, or omit the passage.
- **Generic scale or notability claims:** lists of media coverage, followers, or broad adoption without explaining what those facts establish. Give relevant context or cut the list.
- **False agency:** data, markets, complaints, or culture "decide," "tell us," "reward," or "become" things. Name the people and mechanism when known.

## Formulaic structure and rhythm

- **Binary contrast and negative listing:** "It's not X, it's Y," "not just X but Y," or "not X, not Y, but Z." State the useful claim directly. Keep a contrast when the distinction is real and clarifies the point.
- **Canned persuasion formulas:** "In a world where...", "most people do X, the few who win do Y," "stop X, start Y," "if you aren't doing X you're behind," "the real work isn't X, it's Y," "you don't need X, you need Y," and "it's never been easier/harder." Replace the formula with the specific consequence or action.
- **Faux-reveal and throat-clearing hooks:** "Here's the truth," "what nobody tells you," "the part everyone misses," "honestly?", "look," or "let me be clear" when they only delay a routine claim. Start with the claim. Preserve a real conversational aside.
- **Rhetorical scaffolding:** "What if I told you?", "think about it," "here's what I mean," self-answered question/answer pairs, and signposts such as "let's dive in" or "without further ado." Remove the announcement and say the thing.
- **Dramatic fragments and fake punchlines:** strings of clipped sentences, repeated "Not X. Not Y. Z," or a final mic-drop metaphor. Keep a short sentence that earns emphasis; smooth only the manufactured run or kicker.
- **Rule of three:** ideas repeatedly forced into three-part lists or parallel slogans. Use the number of items the idea actually needs.
- **Robotic symmetry:** repeated sentence openings, identical paragraph shapes, metronomic mid-length sentences, or every paragraph ending with a punchline. Vary rhythm naturally; do not add randomness.
- **Summary-recap endings:** "In conclusion," generic optimism, "the future looks bright," or a last paragraph that repeats the article. End with the final useful fact, implication, or next step.
- **Formulaic outlook sections:** generic "Challenges and future prospects" sections that list stock problems and then say the subject will thrive. Give specific obstacles and real responses or omit the section.
- **Diff-anchored prose:** describing a thing mainly as a change from an earlier implementation when the document should stand alone. Describe how it works now. Keep change framing in changelogs and migration guides.
- **Fragmented headers:** a heading followed by a filler sentence that merely repeats it. Begin with the useful detail.
- **False ranges:** "from X to Y" when the endpoints are not meaningful ends of a scale. List the actual subjects or dimensions.

## Word choice and sentence construction

- **AI-coded or corporate vocabulary:** words such as *delve, realm, harness, unlock, tapestry, paradigm, cutting-edge, revolutionize, landscape, intricate, crucial, pivotal, leverage, synergy, innovative, game-changer, testament, meticulous, groundbreaking, foster, enhance, holistic, garner, transformative, seamless, optimize, scalable, robust, empower, streamline, elevate, proactive, disruptive, reimagine, agile, intuitive, automate, accelerate, dynamic, efficient, immersive, predictive, integrated, turnkey, future-proof, AI-powered, next-generation, ever-evolving, multifaceted, beacon, embark,* and *supercharge*. Also watch abstract uses of *key, valuable, interplay, highlight, emphasize, showcase,* and *align*. These words are not automatically wrong; replace them when a plain, specific word says more.
- **Empty intensifiers and adverbs:** *really, just, literally, genuinely, honestly, simply, actually, deeply, truly, fundamentally, inherently, inevitably, interestingly, importantly,* and *crucially*, along with unnecessary adverbs generally. Delete only when they add no emphasis, uncertainty, contrast, or voice.
- **Business jargon:** *navigate challenges, unpack, lean into, double down, deep dive, take a step back, moving forward, circle back, on the same page,* and *utilize*. Prefer the ordinary verb if it carries the same meaning.
- **Filler:** *it's worth noting, it's important to note, at the end of the day, when it comes to, at its core, in today's world, in the age of, in the world of, the reality is, the truth is, in terms of, with regard to, in order to, going forward,* and *in this article*. Cut when they postpone the point; keep a phrase that carries the author's actual rhythm.
- **Superficial participle analysis:** trailing clauses such as "highlighting the team's commitment," "reflecting its importance," or "showcasing a deep connection" that claim meaning without adding evidence. Explain the concrete relationship or stop at the fact.
- **Copula avoidance and fake-strong verbs:** "serves as," "stands as," "boasts," "features," or "offers" where plain "is" or "has" is clearer. Use a more specific verb when it says what the subject actually does.
- **Synonym cycling:** calling the same person or thing "the agent," "the assistant," "the tool," and "the system" solely to avoid repetition. Repeat the accurate term.
- **Passive or subjectless phrasing:** "mistakes were made" or "results are preserved automatically" can hide the actor. Name the actor when known and relevant. Do not invent one.
- **Excessive hedging:** several layers of *could, potentially, possibly, might,* around a simple claim. Keep real uncertainty; remove the padding.
- **Overused hyphenation:** compounds often need a hyphen before a noun ("a high-quality report") but not after it ("the report is high quality"). Follow standard usage and the document's style rather than imposing a blanket rule.
- **Sentence-starter tics:** repeated openers such as "What makes this..." or paragraphs repeatedly beginning "So" or "Look." Vary only when the repetition is noticeable and not purposeful.
- **Noun piles and weak verb phrases:** "made a decision" or "has the ability to process" can often become "decided" or "can process." Keep the longer phrase when it is more exact.

## Formatting and chatbot residue

- **Em/en dashes:** clusters or decorative dashes can create a mannered cadence. In short copy, usually choose a period, comma, parentheses, or a rewrite. In longer prose, retain a dash if it is the writer's voice and genuinely improves the sentence. Honor an explicit style rule when one applies.
- **Overformatted emphasis:** bold on ordinary nouns, emoji headings, and bold-label bullets repeated down a list. Use formatting only when it helps scanning or follows the publication's conventions. Convert labels to prose only when prose reads better.
- **Title Case headings:** sentence case is a good default; follow the publication's house style and preserve proper nouns.
- **Curly quotes:** not a reliable AI tell. Follow the document's typography rather than changing quotation marks on this basis alone.
- **Chatbot artifacts in prose:** "Great question," "Of course," "I hope this helps," "let me know if you'd like..." or "should I continue?" Remove only when the passage is meant to be standalone copy. Keep the natural greeting or sign-off in a real email, letter, or conversation.
- **Sycophancy:** repeated praise such as "You're absolutely right" before substance. Acknowledge a useful point directly without flattery.

## Signs to preserve

Protect unusual concrete detail, era-specific language, defensible first-person choices, mixed feelings, honest uncertainty, genuine asides, self-corrections, humor, profanity, and changes in pace. These can make writing recognizably personal. Do not "clean up" a writer into a generic polished voice.
