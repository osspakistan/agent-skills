# Principles (consolidated)

## Where sources agree
- **Short**: YC says under 60 seconds; the 5-rule slide says 5-8 sentences max; YC's tighter version is three sentences (what you do, why it's exciting, what you want).
- **Say what you do first**, plain words, no jargon.
- **Lead with best proof**: launch, growth, market, technical team, paid pilots.
- **One explicit ask**, on its own line.
- **No flattery, no backstory** (no resume, awards, origin story).
- **Write like a founder, not an AE**: talk like you would to a friend; read it aloud.

## Conflicts and resolution
| Topic | Positions | Resolution |
|---|---|---|
| Personalization | OpenVC: skip the hook. Thread: keep it human, depends on the investor. YC: no flattery, but find an "uncommon commonality". Slide: personalize, stay on topic. | Personalize only with a reason that can't be pasted onto another recipient. Otherwise skip. |
| Meeting ask | Common founder guides ask for 30 min. YC: don't ask for a meeting, let them escalate. | Ask small: reply, advice, intro, permission to send a demo or deck. |
| Automation | YC: send them yourself at first. Reply: SDR-sent emails lose the "sender is the proof" effect. | Founder-sent can be terser. Non-founder senders need stronger proof and borrowed credibility. |
| Hook | Hormozi: nail the hook first. OpenVC/YC: skip the personalized hook, no flattery. | Open with a pain or outcome line, not a flattering personal one. |
| Offer mechanics | Hormozi: stack bonuses, urgency, guarantees. YC: short, one goal. | Compress the offer to 1-2 sentences; urgency and guarantees only if true. See `offer-and-sales.md`. |
| Musk corpus | Terse, numbers, deadlines. | Borrow tone only; those emails were mostly internal or warm. |

## Full YC list, grouped
**Length & shape**: short (60s); three sentences; one goal per email, cut any word that doesn't serve it; ask on its own line before sign-off.
**Content**: say what you do first; lead with best proof; make the ask explicit (advice, investment, intro); skip backstory; don't flatter; make it about them (their problem, their words); borrow credibility (YC, past employers, customer names).
**Voice & format**: write like you talk to a friend; casual short subject line; send from a company email with your name in it.
**Targeting & process**: find an uncommon commonality (a real reason it's them); target better, not wider (100 good-fit beat 1,000 random); send them yourself, no automation at first; follow up 2-4 times a few days apart; don't get mad if ignored, check back in a few months.
**Added in replies**: deliverability (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) can silently kill a perfect email.

## Common claim-level failures
- Unsourced market sizes ("$18B per year")
- "Proven" anything from an MVP
- "Fully automated" with no scope
- Credentials listed without relevance
- The product's actual function never stated
